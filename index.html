Rajan Sharma, CPA

Fiscal Integrity. Public Accountability. Community Leadership.

Rajan Sharma is a Chartered Professional Accountant and Senior Field Auditor with the Ontario Ministry of Finance, where he manages complex audits of multi-billion-dollar corporations. A member of the Ontario Public Service since 1991, he has served in the Ministries of Health, the Attorney General and Finance, with strict political neutrality and a focus on fiscal oversight and statutory compliance. Before joining the public service, he spent six years in the private sector as a senior accountant, financial analyst and tax analyst with multinational companies, including Nestlé.

Mr. Sharma immigrated to Canada from New Delhi, India, in 1981. For the past 25 years he has matched his professional service with civic leadership across the Greater Toronto Area and Waterloo Region, mentoring more than 70 newcomers and international students, coordinating cultural programming, serving as Secretary and Treasurer of the Milliken Mills Lions Club, and founding the non-profit Bollywood Ki Dhun. He lives in Markham with his wife, Usha Sharma, and their daughters, Diviya Sharma and Drishti Sharma.

Current role

Senior Field Auditor, Employer Health Tax, Compliance Branch, Ontario Ministry of Finance (1998–present). His audits identify systemic compliance gaps and recover public revenue in support of provincial services, and he works strictly within statutory confidentiality protocols.

  • Complex corporate tax audits: He independently plans and carries out detailed field audits of multi-billion-dollar corporations. Each audit works through the company’s accounting systems, financial statements and complex transactions to confirm that it complies with the law and that Ontario collects the revenue it is owed.
  • Interpreting tax law: He interprets and applies federal and provincial tax statutes, including the Income Tax Act, the Corporations Tax Act and the Taxation Act, 2007. He identifies tax avoidance schemes and prepares the working papers and reassessment proposals that support his findings.
  • Resolving disputes: He negotiates technical audit findings with corporate executives, legal counsel and outside CPAs to resolve disputed reassessments. He also works with other tax jurisdictions and mentors junior auditors.

Essay Rajan Sharma, CPA

Beyond the Ledger: Why Cultural Spaces are Vital Civic Infrastructure

What does a Bollywood legend have to do with public finance? Everything the numbers leave out.

For more than 35 years in the Ontario Public Service, and more than half a decade in private industry before that, I have earned my living by making sure things add up. As a tax auditor, I work through large corporate ledgers line by line. In that world, ambiguity is a risk to be flagged, and sentiment has no column.

So it may seem strange that, as Oct. 13 approaches, my thoughts have turned towards a singer. This date marks the anniversary of the death of Kishore Kumar in 1987. Kishore Kumar was the voice of Hindi cinema's golden age. He was a playback singer, actor, director, producer, lyricist, and composer. He was a restless, playful talent whose songs are still sung at family gatherings from Mumbai to Markham.

I have spent much of my own life singing them. Through live tribute concerts and albums, I have performed his music for audiences across the GTA, where some have taken to calling me the "Kishore Kumar of Toronto", a title I accept with more gratitude than accuracy. What does a Bollywood legend have to do with public finance? Everything the numbers leave out.

Over my career, I have watched Ontario transform. Industries have risen and fallen, and newcomers from around the world have become a central engine of the province's growth. In my line of work, immigrant communities show up as figures: businesses incorporated, payrolls remitted, taxes paid. Those figures are real. They help pay for our hospitals, schools and roads. But they measure transactions, not belonging. A province can look prosperous on paper and still be a collection of strangers. What turns strangers into neighbours is harder to audit, and it is what I have spent my evenings and weekends trying to build.

For decades, I have organized community initiatives with the ‘Lions Club’, ‘South Asians in Ontario’, ‘Arya Samaj Markham’ and other GTA organizations. I served as principal lead in purchasing and maintaining a cultural and community centre in Waterloo Region, and learned firsthand that a shared space is a long-term commitment: a mortgage to carry, a roof to fix, volunteers who must keep showing up.

Through my organization, ‘Bollywood Ki Dhun’, more than 300 people gathered at the ‘Vedic Cultural Centre’ in Arya Samaj Markham for a charity event marking what would have been Kishore Kumar's 93rd birthday. Watching Canadians of many backgrounds sing along to Zindagi Ka Safar (‘life's journey’) I saw something no ledger records: a shared heritage turning into generosity and civic joy.

Here is the point I would put to policy-makers. Governments in Canada are good at counting what immigrants contribute economically. We are far less good at valuing the places and practices that make integration last: the community hall, the volunteer-run music program, the charity concert. Too often these are treated as private pastimes. They are civic infrastructure, and when we talk about investing in integration, they belong alongside language training and credential recognition.

Young people are where this matters most. Through ‘Bollywood Ki Dhun’, we mentor young Canadian and Indian artists in music and dance. Off the stage, I have personally mentored more than 70 international students by helping them with housing searches and job hunts, connecting them with immigration information sessions and, when a sudden emergency hits, offering small interest-free loans.

A young student or performer who feels supported both practically and culturally learns she doesn't have to choose between her roots and her new home. That confidence is the foundation of belonging, and belonging is what keeps people invested in a country.

This work needs a long horizon, which suits an auditor. We have already begun planning for Kishore Kumar's centenary in 2029. Cultural preservation, done properly, is like any sound investment: planned years ahead, maintained with discipline and judged by what it returns to the next generation.

One of Kishore Kumar's best-loved songs is titled ‘Zindagi Ek Safar Hai Suhana’ (‘life is a beautiful journey’). Public service, at its best, exists to make that journey better for everyone. Balanced books are part of that. So is a full hall on a Saturday night, singing together.
